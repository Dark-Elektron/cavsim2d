    Three ways to compute the external Q¶

    A mode whose frequency lies above a beam-pipe cutoff does not stay in the cavity. It propagates out along the pipe, and the rate at which it does is the external quality factor \(Q_{\rm ext}\). An eigenmode solve with closed pipe ends cannot see this: the end wall reflects the wave straight back. To get \(Q_{\rm ext}\) the pipe has to end in something that lets the energy leave, and cavsim2d can do that three ways. Two of them are boundary conditions, set like any other in eigenmode_config['boundary_conditions'].

    method

    how the energy leaves

    how to run it

    \(Q_{\rm ext}\) column

    PML

    a complex-stretched absorbing layer on each pipe end

    boundary_conditions='oo'

    Q_eig []

    waveguide ports

    the exact modal impedance of the pipe, a transparent boundary

    boundary_conditions='port'

    Q_ext []

    lossy absorber

    a physical absorbing cone in the pipe, ends closed

    cav.add_dielectric(..., tan_delta=...)

    Q_eig []

    The PML is the default open boundary. The three share nothing but the cavity, so where they agree the answer is not an artefact of any one of them, and where they disagree the reason is worth knowing. This example solves the same TESLA 3-cell cavity all three ways up to 5 GHz, for the monopole and the dipole modes.

PIPES = (0.20, 0.30)            # the two beam pipe lengths [m]
F_MAX = 5000                  # analysis band [MHz]
MESH = {'h': 12, 'p': 3}
POLS = ['monopole', 'dipole']
N_MODES = 70                    # per polarisation: enough to pass F_MAX


def pipe_cutoffs(m):
    """TM_mn and TE_mn cutoffs of the beam pipe below F_MAX [MHz]."""
    x = list(jn_zeros(m, 3)) + list(jnp_zeros(m, 3))
    f = sorted(xi * C0 / (2 * np.pi * R_PIPE * 1e-3) * 1e-6 for xi in x)
    return [fi for fi in f if fi < F_MAX]


CUTOFFS = {0: pipe_cutoffs(0), 1: pipe_cutoffs(1)}
for m, name in ((0, 'monopole'), (1, 'dipole')):
    print(f'{name:9} pipe cutoffs below {F_MAX:.0f} MHz: '
          + ', '.join(f'{f:.0f}' for f in CUTOFFS[m]))


def tesla(name, pipe_length):
    cav = EllipticalCavity(3, MIDCELL, ENDCELL_L, ENDCELL_R, beampipe='both', name=name)
    cav.set_workspace(os.path.join(WORK, name))
    cav.beampipe_length = pipe_length
    return cav


def solve(name, pipe_length, n_modes=N_MODES, cav=None, **config):
    """Solve both polarisations of the cavity with this pipe length."""
    cav = cav or tesla(name, pipe_length)
    cav.eigenmode.run(dict({'polarisation': POLS, 'n_modes': n_modes,
                            'beampipe_length': pipe_length, 'mesh_config': MESH},
                           **config))
    return cav

    The monopole has two cutoffs here, not one. A monopole TM mode can only radiate into the TM\(_{01}\) pipe mode and a monopole TE mode only into TE\(_{01}\), whose cutoff is higher. A dipole mode radiates into TE\(_{11}\), the lowest pipe mode of all, and above the second cutoff into TM\(_{11}\) as well.

    1. Telling cavity modes from pipe modes¶

    Every method returns two kinds of mode above a cutoff: resonances of the cavity, and standing waves of the pipe and its termination that happen to leak. They look alike in a results table. The test that separates them is the same for all three methods: solve again with a longer pipe. A cavity mode does not care how long the pipe is, so its frequency stays put; a pipe mode moves. cav.eigenmode.classify_modes(other) does the matching and returns the verdict with the evidence behind it. It is the best discriminant available, and it is not perfect: a few per cent of modes can be misclassified either way.

    Q is only a tie-breaker in that test. The \(Q\) of a mode close to a cutoff converges slowly with pipe length, because the termination still reaches its evanescent tail, so a genuine mode may keep its frequency while its \(Q\) drifts.

    2. PML: an absorbing layer on each pipe end¶

    boundary_conditions='oo' attaches a perfectly matched layer to both pipe ends. The eigenvalue becomes complex, and its imaginary part is the rate at which the mode loses energy through the pipes:

    \[Q_{\rm ext} = \frac{{\rm Re}\,\omega}{2\,|{\rm Im}\,\omega|}.\]
    pml = [solve(f'pml_{int(p * 1e3)}', p, boundary_conditions='oo') for p in PIPES]

    3. Waveguide ports¶

    boundary_conditions='port' terminates each pipe in its own modal impedance, with n_port_modes (default 3) TM and TE pipe modes of the solve’s azimuthal order at each end: TM\(_{01}\) to TM\(_{03}\) and TE\(_{01}\) to TE\(_{03}\) in the monopole run, TE\(_{11}\) to TE\(_{13}\) and TM\(_{11}\) to TM\(_{13}\) in the dipole run, twelve per run. A mode of order \(m\) couples only to pipe modes of the same \(m\), so TE\(_{11}\) belongs to the dipole run and has no overlap with a monopole mode. Up to 5 GHz TM\(_{01}\) and TE\(_{01}\) propagate in the monopole run, TE\(_{11}\) and TM\(_{11}\) in the dipole run; the rest are evanescent. Below its cutoff a pipe mode does not propagate and the port adds only a reactance, so a trapped mode is pulled in frequency but not damped. That is exact.

    Above a cutoff the port impedance depends on the frequency being solved for, which makes the eigenproblem nonlinear. The solver evaluates the impedance at each mode’s closed-cavity frequency and solves once. The run warns about every mode this affects; section 6 shows what it costs.

    ports = [solve(f'ports_{int(p * 1e3)}', p, boundary_conditions='port') for p in PIPES]

    4. A lossy absorber in the pipe¶

    The third way needs no special boundary: put a real absorber in each pipe and close the ends. Here it is a solid cone of lossy ceramic, full-bore at the end wall and tapering to a point on the axis, so that no wave slips past it. The damping is then ordinary dielectric loss, solved by the complex eigensolver.

    The absorber brings resonances of its own. The field tells them apart directly: every run with a material region reports U_frac_<region> [], the share of the mode’s stored electric energy inside that region. A cavity mode keeps almost all of its energy in the vacuum; a resonance of the absorber lives in the ceramic.

    CONE = {'length': 100.0, 'eps_r': 10.0, 'tan_delta': 0.3, 'maxh': 4.0}   # mm


def absorber_cavity(pipe_length):
    cav = tesla(f'absorber_{int(pipe_length * 1e3)}', pipe_length)
    pipe_end = max(z for z, _ in cav.profile(beampipe_length=pipe_length).points) * 1e3
    for side, name in ((+1, 'absorber_right'), (-1, 'absorber_left')):
        base = side * pipe_end
        cav.add_dielectric(name, CONE['eps_r'], tan_delta=CONE['tan_delta'],
                           maxh=CONE['maxh'],
                           points=[(base - side * CONE['length'], 0.0),
                                   (base, R_PIPE), (base, 0.0)])
    return cav


absorber = [solve(None, p, n_modes=90, cav=absorber_cavity(p),
                  boundary_conditions='mm') for p in PIPES]
absorber[0].plot('geometry', mirror=True)

    The field and the eigenvalue agree on how much of each mode lives in the absorber, to a few per cent: the share integrated from the field, and \(1/(Q\tan\delta)\) from the loss.

    And there are absorber resonances above the cutoffs, not only below them. Below the cutoffs they are unmistakable: 60 to 99 % of their energy sits in the ceramic and their \(Q\) is 3 to 6. Above a cutoff they mix with the pipe and are harder to see without the field. A dipole pair at 2264 MHz, just above the TE\(_{11}\) cutoff, keeps 41 % of its energy in the ceramic; a dipole pair at 2660 MHz keeps 20 %, and a monopole pair at 4511 and 4523 MHz 23 %. In what follows a mode with more than 20 % of its energy in the absorber is counted as the absorber’s. The cut is a judgement, and a mode near it could go either way.

    The absorber also damps the trapped modes: the fundamental passband comes out at \(Q \sim 10^8\) and the monopole modes just below the TM\(_{01}\) cutoff at \(1.3\times10^4\) to \(4\times10^5\), the same order as the copper wall. That is their evanescent tail reaching the cone, a real effect of an absorber 200 mm away, but it is the damping by that absorber, not an external \(Q\).

    6. What each termination does to an empty pipe¶

The ports’ are sharper, with \(Q\) between 9 and 175 (and one on the TE\(_{01}\) cutoff), and so easier to mistake for a cavity mode. For the ports the cause is the single linearised solve: the pipe impedance is frozen at one frequency, so the termination reflects at every other.

    This is why no raw mode list can be trusted above a cutoff, and why the pipe-length test of section 1 is applied to every method.

    7. Monopole modes¶

    RADIATING = 1e6        # a Q_ext above this does not radiate: the mode is trapped
Q_COL = {'PML': 'Q_eig []', 'ports': 'Q_ext []', 'absorber': 'Q_eig []'}


def matched(m):
    # Every radiating cavity mode the PML finds above the first cutoff, and the Q_ext
    # of the other two methods' nearest radiating cavity mode when it is the same
    # resonance: within a quarter of the PML linewidth f/Q, and within 0.5 %.
    pml_m = found['PML']
    pml_m = pml_m[(pml_m['m'] == m) & pml_m['cavity mode'] & (pml_m['Q_eig []'] < RADIATING)
                  & (pml_m['freq [MHz]'] > CUTOFFS[m][0])]
    rows = []
    for _, r in pml_m.iterrows():
        f, q = r['freq [MHz]'], r['Q_eig []']
        row = {'f [MHz]': f, 'R/Q [Ohm]': r['R/Q [Ohm]'], 'PML': q}
        for name in ('ports', 'absorber'):
            d = found[name]
            d = d[(d['m'] == m) & d['cavity mode'] & (d[Q_COL[name]] < RADIATING)]
            row[name] = np.nan
            if len(d):
                j = (d['freq [MHz]'] - f).abs().idxmin()
                shift = abs(d.loc[j, 'freq [MHz]'] - f)
                if shift * q / f < 0.25 and shift / f < 5e-3:
                    row[name] = d.loc[j, Q_COL[name]]
        rows.append(row)
    return pd.DataFrame(rows)

    Agreement follows \(Q\). Where the PML gives a \(Q\) above about 100, the ports reproduce it to about 1 %: two methods that share nothing but the weak form land on the same number, which is good evidence that both are right there. The absorber scatters by up to 25 % around it, higher near 3.3 GHz and lower near 4.5 GHz, which is how the cone’s own reflection varies with frequency. Below a \(Q\) of about 60 the methods disagree, and often cannot even be paired: a mode that broad has no sharp frequency to match on.

    What to take away¶

    • Below every cutoff the three agree that nothing radiates. The ports are exact there; the PML gives a \(Q_{\rm ext}\) far above the wall \(Q_0\); the absorber gives a finite value that is its own damping of the evanescent tail, not an external \(Q\).

    • Above a cutoff every method returns artefacts as well as cavity modes, even for an empty pipe. Solve twice with different pipe lengths and keep what stays put (classify_modes). The test is the best available, and not perfect.

    • Weakly radiating modes (\(Q \gtrsim 100\), most dipole modes here) come out the same from the PML and the ports to about 1 %, and from the absorber to within about 25 %.

    • Strongly radiating modes (\(Q \lesssim 60\), most monopole modes just above the TM\(_{01}\) cutoff) do not. Use the PML, the default, for them. The port result there is a single-pass estimate, and the run says so.

    • Use the absorber to study a real absorber: its damping of trapped modes and its own resonances, which U_frac_<region> [] identifies from the field.
